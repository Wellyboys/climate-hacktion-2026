# ReGround

> **Know where your soil is going before you dig.**

ReGround is a planning, matching and verification system designed to help Auckland construction projects **reuse surplus excavated soil and rock locally** instead of automatically sending suitable material to disposal.

Our core idea is:

> **Plan the destination before excavation → match with a nearby project → verify the actual material → route it to the right place.**

---

# The Problem

Construction and earthworks projects generate large quantities of excavated soil and rock.

Based on the Auckland Council material used in our research, around **2 million tonnes of soil are sent to disposal facilities in Auckland each year**.

Some of this material may still be suitable for use in another construction project.

The problem is that the reuse decision often involves several separate questions:

- What material will actually be excavated?
- How much will be available?
- When will it become available?
- Who nearby needs it?
- Does it meet the receiver's requirements?
- What evidence or testing is required?
- What happens if the material is different from what was expected?

Without this information being coordinated early, projects can end up with:

- stockpiled material,
- repeated handling,
- long-distance transport,
- disposal fees,
- and usable material being treated as waste.

At the same time, another construction project may be purchasing similar fill or soil from somewhere else.

---

# The Existing Process

The construction industry already has services for many individual parts of soil management.

For example:

- contractors organise excavation and transport,
- disposal facilities receive surplus material,
- laboratories and consultants assess material,
- screening operators process soil,
- and material exchange platforms may help advertise available resources.

These services are important.

However, they can operate as **separate steps**.

A project may identify its surplus material only after excavation begins, then search for a destination, organise testing, communicate with a receiver and arrange transport.

A simplified traditional workflow can look like:

```text
Excavate
   ↓
Material becomes surplus
   ↓
Find somewhere to send it
   ↓
Check requirements / testing
   ↓
Arrange transport
   ↓
Reuse or disposal
```

The biggest weakness is that the destination decision can happen **too late in the construction programme**.

---

# What ReGround Changes

ReGround does not try to replace laboratories, geotechnical professionals, contractors or material processors.

Instead, we strengthen the **coordination between these existing services**.

The main improvement is moving the decision earlier.

### Traditional approach

```text
Excavate first
      ↓
Work out what to do with the material
```

### ReGround approach

```text
Forecast material
      ↓
Find potential receiver
      ↓
Agree requirements
      ↓
Excavate
      ↓
Verify actual batch
      ↓
Deliver or reroute
```

This changes the process from a **reactive disposal decision** into a **planned reuse pathway**.

---

# ReGround Pipeline

## 1. Forecast the Surplus

Before excavation begins, information is gathered from:

- project plans,
- geotechnical reports,
- environmental reports,
- expected excavation quantities,
- project location,
- and construction schedules.

This creates an **expected material profile**.

The purpose is not to guarantee exactly what will be underground.

It is to create enough information to begin planning potential destinations before excavation starts.

---

## 2. Match a Receiver

ReGround then searches for nearby projects that require compatible material.

The match considers:

- material type,
- required quantity,
- available quantity,
- timing,
- source location,
- receiver location,
- transport distance,
- and supporting documentation.

For example:

```text
Source project:
420 tonnes available
14–18 October
Silty material
Auckland
        ↓
      MATCH
        ↓
Receiver project:
Needs 300 tonnes
Same time window
Compatible fill requirement
```

This means reuse is not simply based on:

> "Someone nearby wants soil."

Instead it is based on:

> **Does the material, quantity, timing and receiver requirement actually match?**

---

## 3. Agree Acceptance Criteria

Before delivery, the source and receiver establish what conditions must be met.

This may include:

- required soil properties,
- contamination requirements,
- particle-size expectations,
- moisture conditions,
- supporting reports,
- laboratory testing,
- professional review,
- and project-specific approvals.

This stage is important because not every excavated soil or rock material is interchangeable.

A receiver must be able to trust that the delivered material is suitable for its intended purpose.

---

## 4. Verify the Actual Material

Forecasting helps with planning, but the actual excavated material still needs to be checked.

This is where ReGround's proposed physical verification system fits into the workflow.

Possible checks include:

- particle-size fractions,
- mass,
- moisture,
- visual consistency,
- and unusual material characteristics.

The physical measurements are then connected to a **digital batch record** containing information such as:

- source project,
- batch ID,
- measurements,
- documents,
- receiver requirements,
- and routing status.

ReGround does **not** replace professional geotechnical or environmental testing.

Its role is to improve the connection between the original plan and the material that is actually excavated.

---

## 5. Route the Material

After the batch is checked, the material can move into one of three pathways.

| Status | Action |
|---|---|
| **PASS** | Deliver to the agreed receiving project |
| **HOLD** | Further testing or professional review |
| **FAIL** | Find another destination or disposal option |

This gives projects a clear fallback process instead of treating every mismatch as an unexpected problem.

> **Where is this material going before we dig it up?**

# Why Auckland?

We selected Auckland because construction and earthworks generate a substantial excavated-material stream.

The material produced across Auckland can vary depending on location and geology.

Examples include:

- clay,
- silty clay,
- silt,
- sand,
- gravel,
- volcanic material,
- weathered rock,
- basalt,
- and sedimentary material.

Because these materials have different characteristics and potential uses, ReGround does not assume that all excavated material can be reused in the same way.

Instead, reuse is based on the requirements of each source and receiver.

# 2035 Impact Scenario

We also built a simple scenario model to explore the potential long-term impact of local reuse.

This is important:

> **The 2035 figures are not a prediction of exactly what will happen.**

They are a **scenario based on explicit assumptions** designed to answer:

> "If Auckland's excavated-soil stream grows and ReGround gradually captures part of that stream, what could the transport-emissions impact look like?"

---

## Step 1 — Starting Point

Our model begins in **2026** with:

**2,000,000 tonnes/year**

of Auckland excavated soil.

This is used as the baseline material stream.

---

## Step 2 — Growth to 2035

We assumed the amount of excavated material increases by:

**1.2% per year**

from 2026 to 2035.

The reasoning is that Auckland's population, construction activity and infrastructure requirements are expected to continue growing.

The calculation is:

```text
Future soil =
2026 soil × (1 + annual growth rate)^years
```

For 2035:

```text
2,000,000 × (1.012)^9
≈ 2,226,664 tonnes/year
```

So the model estimates that the relevant material stream grows from:

**2.00 million tonnes in 2026**

to approximately:

**2.23 million tonnes in 2035**

This 1.2% rate is a **modelling assumption**, not a measured official forecast of Auckland excavated-soil growth.

---

# Step 3 — ReGround Adoption

We did not assume ReGround immediately captures a large proportion of the market.

Instead, our scenario uses gradual adoption:

| Year | ReGround Share |
|---|---:|
| 2026 | 1% |
| 2027 | 2% |
| 2028 | 3% |
| ... | ... |
| 2035 | 10% |

This represents an illustrative adoption pathway as the concept is tested, validated and potentially expanded.

Therefore in 2035:

```text
2,226,664 tonnes × 10%
≈ 222,666 tonnes
```

So approximately:

# **222,666 tonnes/year**

are redirected to local reuse in the model.

The remaining approximately:

**2.00 million tonnes/year**

continue through the disposal pathway.

The 10% value is not presented as a guaranteed market share.

It is an assumption used to test the scale of potential impact.

---

# Step 4 — Transport Assumptions

The model compares two routes.

### Business as Usual

We used:

**92 km**

as the disposal route.

This represents the long-distance Auckland example used in our project research.

It should **not** be interpreted as the average haul distance for every Auckland construction project.

### Local Reuse

We assumed a ReGround matched receiver is approximately:

**12 km**

from the source.

The purpose is to represent the idea that a successful local match could substantially reduce transport distance.

Therefore:

```text
Business as usual:
Source → 92 km → disposal

ReGround:
Source → 12 km → nearby receiver
```

The result is driven mainly by this difference in transport distance.

---

# Step 5 — Transport Emissions

Our model uses a heavy-freight emissions factor of:

**0.105 kg CO₂-e per tonne-km**

Transport emissions are calculated as:

```text
Material tonnes
× transport distance
× emission factor
```

For business as usual:

```text
Total soil × 92 km × emissions factor
```

With ReGround:

```text
Reused soil × 12 km
+
Remaining soil × 92 km
```

The model therefore does not assume all material suddenly travels only 12 km.

Only the proportion captured by ReGround moves through the shorter reuse pathway.

---

# 2035 Model Result

Under these assumptions, the model produces approximately:

| Metric | 2035 Scenario |
|---|---:|
| Projected excavated soil | **2,226,664 t/year** |
| ReGround share | **10%** |
| Locally reused material | **222,666 t/year** |
| Business-as-usual transport emissions | **~21,510 t CO₂-e/year** |
| ReGround scenario emissions | **~19,639 t CO₂-e/year** |
| Avoided emissions | **~1,870 t CO₂-e/year** |
| Transport-emissions reduction | **~8.7%** |

The model therefore suggests that if 10% of the modelled material stream is redirected from a 92 km disposal route to a 12 km reuse route, transport emissions could be reduced by approximately:

# **1,870 tonnes CO₂-e per year**

by 2035.

---

# Why Is the Reduction 8.7% if ReGround Captures 10%?

This is an important detail.

ReGround captures **10% of the material**, but it does not eliminate transport for that material.

The reused material still travels approximately **12 km**.

So instead of removing 100% of the emissions associated with that 10%, it reduces the transport distance from:

**92 km → 12 km**

That is why the overall modelled emissions reduction is approximately **8.7%**, rather than exactly 10%.

---

# Model Assumptions and Limitations

To avoid overstating the results, our 2035 analysis should be interpreted as a **scenario model**.

### Main assumptions

| Assumption | Model Value |
|---|---:|
| 2026 material baseline | 2,000,000 t/year |
| Annual material growth | 1.2% |
| ReGround adoption in 2026 | 1% |
| ReGround adoption in 2035 | 10% |
| Disposal distance | 92 km |
| Local reuse distance | 12 km |
| Freight emission factor | 0.105 kg CO₂-e/t-km |

### What the model does not include

The current calculation does not model:

- differences between individual Auckland sites,
- empty-truck return trips,
- traffic conditions,
- different truck types,
- loading and unloading emissions,
- screening energy,
- laboratory testing,
- treatment or processing,
- contamination,
- rejected material,
- full lifecycle emissions,
- or actual customer adoption behaviour.

For this reason, the results should be interpreted as:

> **An illustrative transport-impact scenario rather than a measured forecast.**

# The 15% Circular Material Benchmark

Our analysis also references a **15% circular material-use benchmark**.

This is shown as an external comparison.

It is **not** the assumption used for ReGround's market share.

Our ReGround scenario reaches:

**10% in 2035**

while the 15% figure is shown separately as a broader circular-material benchmark.

These values use different contexts and should not be treated as equivalent targets.

# Business Model

A possible ReGround model combines two services.

### Coordination / Transaction Fee

A fee could be charged when ReGround successfully coordinates a source-to-receiver material pathway.

### Verification Service

A separate service fee could apply when on-site physical verification is required.

However, a real commercial pilot would need to measure the complete economics, including:

- disposal fees,
- transport,
- screening,
- testing,
- handling,
- mobilisation,
- material purchasing costs,
- and ReGround fees.
